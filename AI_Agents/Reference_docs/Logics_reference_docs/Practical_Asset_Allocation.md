# Ask PI · Practical Asset Allocation Thesis

*Why we translate the ideal portfolio into the portfolio you can actually own — and what we do about the holdings we cannot trade*

## The one-line thesis

The ideal allocation is a clean answer to *how* to invest a fresh rupee. The practical allocation is the messier, more honest answer to *how* to invest the rupees you already own — starting from the debt funds you already hold for near-term goals, and setting your tax-saving units apart when we place a lump sum. We plan across your mutual funds; direct stocks and PMS sit outside the plan. We take the ideal targets, start from what you already hold rather than from a blank slate — the debt funds you own count toward your near-term goals, and locked tax-saving units are set apart when we place a lump sum — and produce a plan that fits the geometry of what you actually hold today.

## Seven principles that drive every practical recommendation

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **1. The ideal portfolio is the *destination*, not the starting line** | Every practical allocation begins from the ideal one: the same near-term protection and long-term growth split, and, like the ideal plan, no separate emergency reserve by default. That clean target is the destination. The practical layer only *re-shapes the path* so the destination is reachable from the portfolio you hold today, without sells we don't want to make. |
| **2. No new money into tax-saving (ELSS) funds** | ELSS units carry a statutory 3-year lock-in, so we never put fresh money into ELSS — no SIP, lump sum or rebalancing buy goes there. When we work out where a lump sum should go, and when we rebalance, the ELSS units you hold are set apart as frozen: their value is *counted* toward your long-term equity so the plan still meets its growth target, they take no new money, and a rebalance never trades them. Your main plan values ELSS units alongside your other mutual funds rather than setting them apart. |
| **3. We plan your mutual funds; direct stocks sit outside the plan** | Single-stock and PMS holdings can't be managed the precise, tax-efficient way mutual-fund units can, and stock-picking is not our mandate. So we plan across your mutual funds only: your stocks are not counted toward your targets, and we never recommend buying or selling them. Your plan's equity share is therefore for your mutual funds alone — if you also hold stocks, your overall equity exposure is higher than the plan shows. |
| **4. The same growth-fund discipline, applied to what's left** | After the frozen holdings are accounted for, the remaining growth allocation follows exactly the same sub-strategy as the ideal portfolio — the same eligible equity styles, the same valuation and market-view discipline. Smaller leftover pools naturally consolidate into fewer, more meaningful positions rather than a scatter of slivers. |
| **5. One picture, holdings and all** | The practical output mirrors the ideal one so the two read interchangeably, with an extra line for any locked holdings set apart. You should be able to see, in one place, how your whole corpus is placed — without cross-referencing two documents. |
| **6. Your stated preferences shape this plan — and only this plan** | If you have told us how you want your money invested, this is the step that honours it. Two deliberate limits. First, your preference reshapes the *practical* plan, never the ideal one: our own recommendation is always computed as though you had said nothing, so "here is what we would advise, here is your version" is a real comparison rather than two views of the same adjusted number. Second, a preference is honoured at the point each decision is made, not bolted on at the end — an ask applied after the portfolio is already assembled cannot move the parts that are already fixed, and would quietly land you somewhere other than where you asked. |
| **7. This step decides *targets*, not trades** | The practical engine says where the money should sit. It does not decide which lots to sell, when to realise gains, or how to stagger trades — those are tax-aware decisions that belong to the rebalancing step (see the Rebalancing thesis). |

We are a translation layer — not a tax optimiser, not a fund-picker, and not a substitute for the rebalancer. We take the ideal answer and round it into the shape of what you actually hold, so the rest of the process doesn't have to.

## How a practical allocation is built — at a high level

Every Ask PI practical allocation moves through five deliberate stages. We can walk through the reasoning behind any of them on demand.

### Stage 1 — Start from the ideal near-term plan

Near-term protection follows the rules the ideal thesis describes, and, like the ideal plan, the default plan carves no separate emergency reserve. Locked tax-saving units, where they are set apart, are never used to fund near-term goals.

Near-term goals — those due within roughly the next couple of years, measured from the end of the current financial year, each valued at what it will cost on its date — use the ideal plan's nearest-first rule, but from where you actually stand. The ideal plan assumes you hold nothing yet; here, the debt and arbitrage funds you already hold count first (income-plus-arbitrage funds aside — we treat those as long-term debt), then your monthly SIP, and your savings are set aside only for what the SIP cannot build up by each goal's date. Debt you hold beyond what your goals need is simply part of your long-term plan and is rebalanced like everything else, so it may be moved into equity. If we have no holdings on file for you, we can't count on any savings being moved for your goals yet, so your SIP carries as much of them as it can.

### Stage 2 — Account for the holdings we can't freely move

When locked tax-saving units are set apart — as they are when we place a lump sum or rebalance — they are counted toward your long-term equity target but left untouched; if they alone exceed that target, the equity share rises to hold them. Your main plan values ELSS units alongside your other funds instead (see principle 2).

### Stage 3 — Place the core diversified holding

A diversified, tax-efficient core holding is sized the same way it is in the ideal portfolio, from the growth money that can actually be bought — large enough to do real diversification work and, when we size it ourselves, bounded so it never dominates the growth allocation. If you name a size for it as your preference, we use your size instead of our bound, reduced only if tax-saving units set apart or a refused gold allocation leave too little room for it.

### Stage 4 — Fill in the remaining growth styles

Whatever growth allocation is left after the core and the locked holdings is split across the same eligible equity styles as the ideal portfolio, under the same valuation and market-view discipline. Because this leftover pool can be small when large locked holdings are set apart, it naturally consolidates into fewer meaningful positions.

### Stage 5 — Present one holdings-aware picture

The final output mirrors the ideal allocation, with a breakdown of your corpus into any locked holdings set apart and the part the plan is free to place. The amounts in your plan are worked out to the rupee, so they add up to exactly the corpus the plan covers; the amounts you actually invest or move are rounded to clean ₹100 figures.

## What stating a preference changes

A preference is a real instruction, so we apply it in full — and we are equally plain about what it costs.

**It is set on your preferences page, not in chat.** Asking chat for a different mix gets a pointer to the preferences page, alongside the plan or investment when your message also asks for one. Chat does not rebuild the plan to a passing remark. The page shows where you stand today beside each category, as a share of the mutual-fund holdings a preference can shape — your tax-saving (ELSS) units, whether or not still inside their lock-in, and funds that fit none of the page's categories are shown as a separate excluded share, and direct stocks are not included.

**It is expressed as a share of your whole portfolio.** Asking for a quarter in mid caps means a quarter of everything you hold, not a quarter of some sub-pool you would have to work out for yourself. Refusing a category removes it outright rather than shrinking it to a token holding; if you tell us no gold, no fund that holds gold appears at all — including the diversified multi-asset core, which holds a little gold — and that share moves to equity; the plan tells you when that visibly shifts your mix.

**It changes the order of priorities, not just the mix.** Ordinarily we first earmark money for your near-term goals — those due within roughly the next couple of years, measured from the end of the current financial year — and only then invest what remains. When you set a preference, we stop making that carve-out and invest the whole corpus to the shape you asked for — none of your savings, existing debt or monthly SIP is set aside for near-term goals, so a goal inside that window has no money earmarked for it, and your SIP follows your stated split. That is a genuine trade, so we name it before you commit whenever it applies to *your* situation, and attach the same facts to the plan that follows.

**Where two instructions collide, we say so rather than silently picking one.** Your asset-class split and any locked holdings set apart outrank a category-level ask. The preferences page refuses, when you save, a set of categories that adds up to more than its asset class, and a multi-asset fund too large for your split. When tax-saving units set apart for a lump sum leave less equity room than your categories ask for, those categories are scaled back proportionally and the multi-asset fund is cut to the largest that fits — and the plan tells you that happened. Beyond rounding, nothing is trimmed quietly. A combination that cannot be built at all is refused on the preferences page rather than accepted and quietly dropped: a multi-asset fund always holds some equity, debt and commodity, so it cannot sit in a mix that sets any of those to zero.

## Why a customer should trust this approach

| Question | Our answer |
| --- | --- |
| **How do you treat my ELSS (tax-saving) units?** | Tax-saving (ELSS) units carry a statutory 3-year lock-in, so we never put new money into them. When we place a lump sum or rebalance, the ELSS you hold is counted toward your growth target, everything else is shaped around it, and a rebalance never trades it. Your main plan values ELSS alongside your other mutual funds. |
| **Why doesn't my plan include my direct stocks?** | We plan across your mutual funds only. Single stocks and PMS can't be managed with the same precision and tax efficiency, and picking stocks isn't our mandate — so they are not counted toward your targets, and we never recommend buying or selling them. Your plan's equity share therefore covers your mutual funds alone: if you also hold stocks, your overall equity exposure is higher than the plan shows, which is worth keeping in mind. |
| **Why are my growth holdings smaller than the ideal portfolio suggests?** | Because they're allocated from what remains *after* the money that is already spoken for. Debt and arbitrage funds you already hold go to your near-term goals first, so this plan's near-term pot usually matches or exceeds the ideal one; and when locked holdings are set apart, as they are when we place a lump sum or rebalance, growth funds are sized from what is left. A smaller leftover pool means smaller individual positions even though the overall shape is unchanged. That's the translation working correctly. |
| **Does this step ever recommend a sell?** | No. It decides where money should sit. Which lots to sell, and when, is decided by the tax-aware rebalancing step. |
| **What changes if I add fresh capital next month?** | Your plan is worked out again on what you hold plus the new money. Locked tax-saving units are set apart and take none of it; the fresh capital first covers what your near-term goals still need beyond the debt and arbitrage funds you already hold for them — the whole remaining need, without counting on future SIPs, up to the full amount you add — then goes to the long-term categories where you hold less than your plan's target. If you have set a preference, there is no near-term carve-out, so the whole amount goes to the categories where you hold less than your stated split. |
| **Why does this plan set aside a different amount for my near-term goals than the ideal plan?** | Both use the same nearest-first rule from different starting points. The ideal plan assumes you hold nothing yet, so it counts only your SIP and your savings. This plan counts the debt and arbitrage funds you already hold first, then your SIP, and sets savings aside only for what the SIP can't reach in time. Because debt you already hold is put to work for your nearest goals rather than moved into equity while your SIP rebuilds it, this plan's figure usually matches the ideal one or comes out higher. If you have set a preference, this plan sets nothing aside for near-term goals (see "What stating a preference changes"). |

## What this thesis is — and is not

This document is a directional reference. It is not a tax-planning tool, not a stock-picker, and not a substitute for the actual allocation, which is always personalised to your corpus, your locked units and your goals. The caps and routing rules behind it are reviewed periodically and may evolve. When they do, this thesis is updated and dated.
