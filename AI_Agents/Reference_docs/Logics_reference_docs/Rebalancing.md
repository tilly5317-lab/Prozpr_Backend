# Ask PI · Portfolio Rebalancing Thesis

*Why we change the portfolio when we do — and why we leave it alone the rest of the time*
*Thesis version 1.8 · Internal & client reference · Last updated: 3 October 2026*

---

> **About this document:** This is a directional reference for how we think about rebalancing — the philosophy, not the formula. The exact drift thresholds and ranking bands are proprietary and are deliberately not reproduced here. Statutory tax facts (capital-gains rates, holding-period thresholds) are public and are stated plainly. Rebalancing runs *after* the practical allocation step (see `Practical_Asset_Allocation.md`), which translates your ideal targets into a holdings-aware plan.

## The one-line thesis

A rebalance is a **disciplined return to the plan** — not a reaction to markets, not a punishment for last quarter's laggards, and not a way to look busy. Your portfolio drifts because markets move, contributions arrive, and our recommended-fund list evolves. We bring the portfolio back to the shape we set up for your goals, but with a deliberate bias against unnecessary trading and a strict eye on the tax bill. Rebalancing should make your plan more like the plan — quietly, cheaply, and with the tax-cheapest units going first.

## Eight principles that drive every rebalancing decision

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Rebalance to the plan, not to the market** | The targets we measure against are the goal-based allocation we computed for *you*. We do not raise equity because equity has run, and we do not cut equity because equity has fallen. If your goals and risk profile have not changed, the target shape has not changed, and rebalancing simply pulls the portfolio back onto that shape. |
| **2. Don't trade for trading's sake** | Every trade risks a capital-gains tax bill, and we refuse to pay it for cosmetic alignment. A holding that is already close to its target is left alone. "Close enough" is judged two ways — against your whole portfolio and against the fund itself — and a change is made once it clears either bar, so a large fund isn't left far off target and a small one isn't churned for a sliver. A fund the plan sells entirely always clears it. The bar is a deliberate policy choice, reviewed over time — not folklore. |
| **3. Quality bar trumps comfort** | A fund our research team has explicitly marked for exit — typically because they have replaced it or it failed an ongoing screen — is a must-sell regardless of how small the drift looks. We do not keep a sub-standard fund around just because exiting it is inconvenient or tax-expensive. A fund that has merely slipped off our active recommended list without an exit call is treated more gently — see Step 2. |
| **4. A few strong funds per category, without needless switching** | Each part of your plan is held in our top-ranked fund — or split across the top two once your portfolio is large enough for both to hold a meaningful amount. If you already hold a recommended fund ranked close to the top of that category, you keep it rather than selling a good fund just to buy the one ranked a step higher; only the rest of the target is split across the top funds. You end up in funds we rate highly, without churn for its own sake. |
| **5. Tax-aware selling, cheapest units first** | When we sell — to trim, to exit, or to fund a buy — which units leave is the single biggest lever on the tax bill. We always sell the tax-cheapest units first: long-term lots lead (long-term capital gains are taxed at 12.5% above a ₹1,25,000 annual exemption), and lots sitting on a loss cost no tax to sell (losses offset gains in the tax estimate; where you have asked for a short-term-gains budget, short-term losses can also unlock more rebalancing — see Principle 6). Routine trims go one step further: they only ever touch long-term units — short-term units are sold only when a fund must be exited outright. A fund marked for exit still gets sold even if it's the most tax-expensive — but among funds we *could* sell, the tax-cheap ones always go first. |
| **6. Respect your short-term-gains budget; put losses to work** | A routine trim never realises short-term gains — only a fund that must be exited sells short-term units — so your standard plan sets no cap on them. If you ask how the plan changes when this year's short-term gains stay under an amount you name, we answer on the spot with a version that stops adding new short-term gains once that budget is hit and defers the rest. In a second pass, short-term losses — carried forward from prior years or realised in this run — are pooled, and as much of the deferred selling as those losses can absorb is converted into actual sells. Long-term losses stay out of that pool, because the tax rules let them offset only long-term gains. Losses don't cut tax on past returns, but they unlock additional rebalancing without raising this year's tax bill. |
| **7. The engine never invents cash** | We treat every rebalance as a closed system: every buy is funded from a sell, so total buys never exceed total sells. If demanded buys exceed allowed sells, buys are scaled down proportionally and the shortfall is reported, never hidden. If a must-exit fund raises more cash than those buys need, the rest goes to the funds still short of their targets — biggest gap first — even where that top-up would otherwise be too small to make, so money from an exit is not left idle; anything still left is reported as cash. Fresh money — a SIP or a lump sum — is invested through its own step, straight toward your plan with nothing to sell; a rebalance doesn't pretend to find money that isn't on the table. |
| **8. ELSS stays put; direct stocks sit outside the plan** | We rebalance your mutual funds only. Direct stocks and PMS sit outside our plan, and we never recommend buying or selling individual stocks. Tax-saving (ELSS) funds get no fresh money in a rebalance, and the ELSS units you hold are never traded: they count toward your growth target and stay as they are. |

We are a rebalancing approach, not a market-timing engine. We do not raise cash before a perceived correction, lever up before a perceived rally, or chase whichever subgroup is in vogue. The plan that walks into a rebalance is the plan that walks out — better aligned, tax-efficient, and on the right side of every guardrail.

## When you want to steer the plan — comply, and caution

We won't tilt your portfolio to chase a market view — but you may want to shape it for reasons of your own, and that is a different thing entirely. You might want to lean further into equity, favour a category like mid-cap, or keep a whole kind of fund out — overseas funds, say. When you tell us, we honour it. This is your money, and we are your advisor, not a gatekeeper — the call is yours to make.

**Where you tell us matters.** A standing instruction about how your money is invested belongs on your investment preferences page, not in passing conversation — so if you raise one mid-chat, we point you there rather than quietly rebuilding the plan around a remark. It takes one extra moment, and in exchange the instruction is deliberate, visible, and applied to *every* plan we build afterwards rather than to a single reply you may never see again. Asks that aren't standing instructions — *"what if I put in another ₹5 lakh"*, a corrected tax rate — are still answered on the spot, in the same turn.

What we won't do is let you decide blind. Your preferences page shows our own recommendation and your holdings today beside your choice. Once you save, every plan built on your preference says so: it quotes the preference back to you, says plainly where the plan could not follow it all the way (including that we are not setting money aside for near-term goals), and shows the tax it sets in motion. Every plan built on your preference says so, quoting it back to you, states plainly where the plan could not follow it all the way, and shows the tax it sets in motion. Our own recommendation is always computed as if you had said nothing, precisely so that comparison stays honest. You get the plan you asked for *and* a clear-eyed view of the trade-off, every time. Comply, and caution — never one without the other.

And where we genuinely cannot honour a request in full, we say so plainly rather than pretend. If the categories you set together want more than their asset class holds, the preferences page tells you before you save rather than trimming them quietly. Ask in chat to favour a kind of fund we don't research and we point you to your preferences page, which lists only the categories we research. The ask itself is not applied to the plan, and we won't manufacture a view we don't hold. An honest "not here" is worth more than a confident answer we can't stand behind.

## How a rebalance is built — at a high level

Every Ask PI rebalance is the output of a holdings-aware pre-stage and six deliberate, auditable steps. We can walk through the reasoning behind any of them on demand.

### Step 0 — Practical pre-stage: translate ideal into a holdings-aware plan

Before anything is traded, the practical allocation step translates your ideal targets into the portfolio you actually hold, planning across your mutual funds only — direct stocks and PMS sit outside the plan. It also sets how much your near-term goals need in short-term debt: the debt and arbitrage funds already in the portfolio being rebalanced count first, then your monthly SIP, and savings are moved only for what the SIP can't build up by each goal's date. Debt beyond what those goals need is part of your long-term plan and is rebalanced like everything else. (A saved investment preference suspends this near-term carve-out.) The output of that step is what this engine works from. See `Practical_Asset_Allocation.md`.

### Step 1 — Decide which funds hold each part of the plan

Each asset subgroup in your plan maps to one or more recommended funds, ordered by rank. A recommended fund you already hold, ranked close to the top of its subgroup, keeps what it holds; the rest of the subgroup's target goes to the top-ranked fund, or is split equally across the top two once your portfolio is large enough. Sizing only uses funds on our recommended list — we never invent new positions.

### Step 2 — Compare to present holdings: hold, top up, trim or exit

We join the targets to your current holdings and label each fund: hold, top up, trim or exit. A change too small to matter — judged against your whole portfolio and against the fund itself — emits no action, and a fund the plan sells entirely always clears that bar; the recommendation lists only what is actually changing. One situation overrides that: a fund our research team has explicitly marked for exit is exited regardless of how small the drift looks. A fund you hold that is merely no longer on our active recommended list sits in between: it gets no fresh money, its long-term units migrate to recommended funds when buys need funding, and its short-term units are left alone.

Debt follows one more rule: we never sell a debt fund you hold just to buy another debt fund. Every planned debt sale is first set against the plan's debt buys — across every kind of debt fund, including ones no longer on our recommended list — and where the two match, your existing fund is kept, the buy shrinks, and the plan notes how much it kept. A debt fund marked for exit is still exited, and debt with no matching debt buy — more than your plan needs — can still be sold to fund other parts of the plan, such as equity.

### Step 3 — Classify every lot for tax

Before any sell, every lot of every sell-candidate fund is bucketed by tax behaviour: long-term vs short-term (12 months for equity funds; 24 months for debt funds our records classify as debt — a fund we have not classified is treated as equity), and realised gain vs realised loss. This is the input the tax-aware sell ordering works from.

### Step 4 — First pass: trade within your short-term-gains budget

We walk sells tax-cheapest first (long-term lots, with loss-making lots cheapest of all; short-term units are touched only on forced exits). Your standard plan sets no short-term-gains budget; where you have asked for one, we stop adding new short-term gains once it is used up, and the blocked selling is deferred, not lost. Buys are funded from the resulting sell pool, scaled down proportionally if they exceed it. A must-exit fund is sold whatever the buys need — in full, unless a budget you set holds back some of its newer units; if it raised more than they need, the rest tops up the funds still short of target, biggest gap first, even where that top-up is smaller than our usual bar.

### Step 5 — Second pass: put losses to work

Short-term losses carried forward from prior years, plus short-term losses realised in the first pass, form a loss-offset budget; long-term losses stay out of it, because the tax rules let them offset only long-term gains. We take the selling a short-term-gains budget deferred and convert as much of it as those losses can absorb into actual sells — additional rebalancing without raising this year's tax bill. The same short-term losses also offset the short-term gains in every plan's tax estimate.

### Step 6 — Presentation: trades, totals and rationale

We output the final trade list, each line with a plain-English reason, plus totals (gross buys and sells, realised gains and losses, an estimated tax bill) and any notes that shape the picture — a fund on our exit list, debt kept rather than switched, newer units held back from a sale. Alongside the trades, the plan shows your goal mix, where this plan lands and where you are today; when the plan stops noticeably short of your goal mix, it says by how much and why. Every changed line traces back to a documented rule.

## Why a customer should trust this approach

| Question | Our answer |
| --- | --- |
| **You're not touching fund X — shouldn't we rebalance it?** | Its gap to target is within our "close enough" bar — too small to matter against your whole portfolio or against the fund itself. Trading it would risk a capital-gains tax bill for cosmetic precision. We act when it matters; we don't trade for the sake of trading. |
| **Why are you exiting fund Y? It seems to be doing fine.** | Our research team has explicitly marked it for exit — typically because they have replaced it or it failed an ongoing screen. That is a quality call independent of recent performance, and it overrides the "close enough" bar by design. |
| **Why are you buying a small amount of fund V — isn't that below your bar?** | It is, and on its own we wouldn't make it. But a fund you held had to be exited, and its money needs a home: rather than leave it as idle cash, we top up the funds still short of their targets, biggest gap first. |
| **Fund W isn't on your recommended list any more — why aren't you exiting it?** | Not every off-list fund is a must-sell. Unless our research has marked it for exit, we simply stop adding to it: its long-term units migrate to recommended funds when buys need funding, and its short-term units stay put so you don't pay avoidable short-term tax on a fund that's still fine to hold. A debt fund goes one step further: it is never sold just to buy another debt fund, so it keeps counting toward your debt target unless your plan needs less debt overall. |
| **Why didn't you move my debt fund into your recommended one?** | Because switching one debt fund for another leaves what you actually hold unchanged and can trigger a tax bill. We set every planned debt sale against the plan's debt buys and keep your existing fund where they match. A debt fund marked for exit is still exited, and if you hold more debt than your plan needs, the excess can still move into other parts of your plan. |
| **Why are you selling my oldest units first?** | Tax. Long-term gains are taxed at 12.5% above a ₹1,25,000 annual exemption — usually the cheapest units to liquidate — and units sitting on a loss are cheaper still, because losses offset gains. For a routine trim we stop there: short-term units are sold only when a fund has to be exited outright. The policy is the same every run. |
| **You wanted to trim fund Z but the trade is smaller — why?** | Usually because a routine trim sells only units you've held long enough to count as long-term (a year for equity funds, two years for debt-style funds) — newer units stay put to avoid short-term tax — and a trim raises only as much as the plan's buys need. If you've asked for a short-term-gains budget, it can also hold back part of an exit; that deferred amount is re-attempted in the second pass against any short-term losses you have. Whatever is still left is reported, not hidden. |
| **Why are my buys smaller than the plan said?** | We fund every buy from a sell — buys never exceed sells. If allowed sells fell short (because most of what the plan would sell hasn't yet been held long enough to count as long-term, or a short-term-gains budget you set held sells back), buys are scaled down proportionally and the shortfall reported. A debt buy can also shrink because a debt fund you already hold was kept in its place. New money — a SIP or a lump sum — is invested through its own step, not through a rebalance. |
| **Why doesn't the plan take me all the way to my goal mix?** | A rebalance adds no new money: every rupee it buys comes from something it sells, and a routine trim sells only units held long enough to count as long-term. A portfolio with a lot of recent money can therefore move only part of the way in one rebalance. The plan shows your goal mix, where this plan lands and where you are today, and when it stops noticeably short it says by how much and why. A SIP or a lump sum is invested toward your plan directly, with nothing to sell — the quicker way to close the rest. |
| **What happens to my tax-saving (ELSS) funds?** | A rebalance puts no new money into ELSS and never sells the ELSS units you hold. ELSS units carry a statutory 3-year lock-in, so the plan counts them toward your growth target and leaves them as they are — units past the lock-in included. |
| **Why doesn't the rebalance cover my direct stocks?** | We rebalance your mutual funds only. Single stocks and PMS sit outside our plan, so we never recommend buying or selling them. Your plan's equity share therefore covers your mutual funds alone — if you also hold stocks, your overall equity exposure is higher than the plan shows. |
| **What if I add money next month — will you re-run?** | Yes. There's no fixed calendar cadence: a plan is built when you first open it or ask for one, rebuilt when you save new investment preferences, and rebuilt automatically in the background when a new statement brings in new transactions — from a fresh allocation and the holdings in that statement. The new money itself is invested through its own SIP or lump-sum plan, straight toward your plan, rather than through a rebalance. |

## What this thesis is — and is not

This document is a directional reference for the *why* of rebalancing decisions. It is not a market call, not a guarantee that trimming a winner will look smart in three months, and not a substitute for the actual trade list, which is always personalised to your holdings, your tax state, and your goals. Thresholds, caps and policies behind it are reviewed periodically and may evolve as markets and tax law change. When they do, this thesis is updated and dated.
