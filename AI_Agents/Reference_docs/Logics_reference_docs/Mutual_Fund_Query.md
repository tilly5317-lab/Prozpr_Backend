# Ask PI · Fund Explainer Thesis

*Why we recommend the funds we do — and why we'll only tell you what we can actually stand behind*
*Thesis version 1.1 · Internal & client reference · Last updated: 3 October 2026*

---

> **About this document:** This is a directional reference for how Pi answers a question about a *specific* mutual fund — why we recommend it, how it has performed, and how it stacks up against comparable funds — and a request to name the best-performing funds. The fund ranking itself and the criteria behind it are proprietary and are deliberately not reproduced here. This step only *explains* funds; deploying money into them is the additional-investment step's job, and swapping existing holdings is the rebalancer's.

---

## The one-line thesis

When you ask about a fund, we answer only from what we can actually back up: our own documented reason for recommending it, and its real historical returns computed from stored NAV data. We would rather tell you we don't have a number than invent one — because a confident wrong figure is worse than an honest gap.

## Six principles that drive every fund answer

| Principle | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **One fund, pinned precisely** | A fund name can map to half a dozen scheme variants (Regular/Direct × Growth/IDCW), each with different NAV, so we always pin one plan and a "return" is never ambiguous. If you hold the fund, we speak to the exact plan you hold (your Regular or Direct plan, with its own costs), so the returns are that plan's track record. They are not your personal return on the money you put in; your own portfolio view shows that. Otherwise we speak to the **Direct-Growth** plan — the standard representative of a fund's track record, and the plan we recommend. If your question could mean two different funds, we ask which one rather than guess. |
| **Returns are real and annualized** | We show one-, three- and five-year returns, each as **CAGR (annualised)**, computed from our stored NAV history — the industry-standard "% per year". If we don't yet hold enough history for a horizon (say a fund younger than five years), we say so plainly instead of estimating. |
| **Peers are like-for-like** | "How does it compare?" means against funds of the **same type** — a flexi-cap against other flexi-caps, not against a bucket that mixes mid-caps and hybrids — drawn from the funds we actually recommend in that category and compared on their three-year annualised return. Because peers come from our shortlist, a fund outside it has no peer set to show; we share its own returns instead. Name two or more funds and we compare them head-to-head with each other rather than adding peers. |
| **Our rank is a house view, not a league table** | When we say a fund is a top pick, we mean **within our own recommended shortlist for the part of your portfolio it fills** — a shortlist that can span more than one fund type, so a flexi-cap fund and a hybrid fund can sit on the same list. We never present it as a market-wide performance percentile like "3rd of 24" — it is our order of preference, not a performance position, and we won't imply one. |
| **If it isn't one we recommend, we say so** | You can ask about any fund, including ones we don't cover. For those we'll share the returns we have, but we'll tell you plainly it isn't one we actively recommend — and we won't manufacture a rationale for it. Our house view is attached to the exact plan on our shortlist, so if you hold a different plan of a shortlisted fund (a Regular plan, say), the answer carries that plan's returns without our house view. |
| **No invented numbers, ever** | Every figure in a fund answer traces to our stored data. We never recompute, round to look cleaner, or attribute a number to "live data" we don't have. |

## Why this shape

A fund recommendation is one of the most consequential things we say. The failure we design against is the *plausible fabrication* — a precise-looking return or ranking that sounds authoritative and is simply made up. Grounding every answer in our ranking and our NAV history, and refusing to fill gaps, is what keeps "why we recommend it" trustworthy.

## What this step covers — and what it doesn't

Four kinds of question sound similar and get answered by four different parts of Pi. The line between them is worth stating, because getting it wrong is the difference between a fact and a recommendation.

| You ask… | Who answers | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *"What are this fund's returns?"* · *"How does it compare to its peers?"* · *"Why do you recommend it?"* · *"Which are the best large-cap funds?"* | **This step** | These are questions about funds — as instruments, whether or not you own them. We answer from our ranking and our stored NAV history. |
| *"Which of **my** funds has done best?"* · *"My top performers"* | Your **portfolio** answer | The possessive is the tell. This is a question about your holdings, answered from your own data — not from our recommended universe. A superlative like "best" doesn't override an explicit "my". |
| *"Do I have the right funds?"* · *"Should I stop this SIP?"* · *"Which of my funds should I get rid of?"* | **Rebalancing** | This asks for a *verdict* and an *action*, not a fact. Answering it honestly means comparing what you hold against what we'd recommend and telling you what to change — which is the rebalancer's job, tax and all. |
| *"Which fund should I put my ₹5 lakh in?"* · *"Which fund is best for **me**?"* | **Additional investment** | Fresh money plus a choice of fund is a deployment decision, not a fact about a fund. That step sizes the money against your plan first, then picks the funds. |

**On "which are the best funds in X?"** — when you ask for a list rather than about a named fund, we rank the funds in our fund database — overall, or within the category you name — by their annualised past return over a stated period (three years unless you name another) and show the top few. Each fund appears once, on its Regular-Growth plan, and every fund is measured over the same period to a common date; funds without a full track record for that period, or that have stopped reporting a current NAV, are left out. The category has to be one of the fund types we cover; for any other, the list covers all funds. This list is ranked purely on past return, with a reminder that past performance doesn't guarantee future returns. It is *not* our recommended shortlist, and not a recommendation to buy: putting money into any of them is the additional-investment step, which weighs the category against your actual plan first.
