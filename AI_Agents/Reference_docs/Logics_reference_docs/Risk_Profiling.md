# Ask PI · Risk Profiling Thesis

*Why we score risk the way we do — and what your number actually means*

## The one-line thesis

Risk is two questions that look like one. *Can you afford to take this risk?* — a function of your age, your savings cushion and what you owe. *Do you want to take this risk?* — a function of your temperament, your experience and your stated comfort with drawdowns. Neither answer by itself builds a real portfolio: a young saver with no appetite will under-invest for decades; an older investor with a large appetite will over-invest into a market they cannot wait out. We assess both, combine them in a way that respects your stated preference while keeping it anchored to your financial reality, and where the two disagree by enough to matter, we record the gap in the written summary stored with your risk profile — a note, not a gate on what happens next.

## Five principles that drive every risk score

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Assess ability and willingness separately** | We form two independent views on the same scale: a **risk-capacity** view (purely financial — age, how long your cash and deposits could cover your expenses, and what you owe) and a **risk-willingness** view (your stated comfort with risk). Keeping them separate lets us tell you *which* of the two is driving your final number — and where the disagreement lies if there is one. |
| **2. Age is the spine of capacity, not the whole skeleton** | A younger investor has decades to ride out a drawdown; someone near retirement does not. Age sets the base of your capacity, tapering as the retirement window approaches. But age alone is too crude — a young investor with no savings cushion and a credit-card balance has less room to ride a drawdown than age suggests, and vice versa. So your broader financial picture lifts or lowers that starting point. |
| **3. Savings discipline is a quiet but powerful signal** | We read the share of income you actually save as evidence of your ability to absorb volatility, and we record it in the written summary stored with your risk profile: strong, consistent saving is acknowledged; saving almost nothing is noted as a caution; a slim-but-positive margin is simply neutral. It does not move the score itself. |
| **4. Willingness leads, capacity tethers** | Your stated preference carries the greater weight in the final score, because the portfolio has to be one *you can actually live with through a sharp drawdown* — a paper-optimal portfolio you panic-sell at the bottom is worse than a paper-suboptimal one you hold. But capacity always tethers the result: a high appetite sitting on a thin financial base will not produce a maximally aggressive score, because your stomach should not override your bank account. |
| **5. Flag the gap, don't paper over it** | When your willingness and your capacity diverge meaningfully, we record it explicitly in the written summary stored with your risk profile, rather than letting it quietly shape your number. It's one of the most useful things we can tell you: a large appetite on a thin base is the profile most likely to abandon the plan at the worst possible time, and the inverse is the profile most likely to leave decades of compounding on the table. |

We are a risk-profiling approach, not a personality test and not a brokerage suitability form. The output is one number, traceable to the factors that produced it, built to inform how your portfolio is shaped — nothing more.

## How a risk score is built — at a high level

Every Ask PI risk profile moves through four deliberate stages. We can walk through the reasoning behind any of them on demand.

### Stage 1 — Capacity from age

We start with age, because time-to-need is the single biggest driver of how much volatility you can afford. Capacity starts higher for younger investors and tapers as the retirement window draws closer — and it tapers fastest through the years right around retirement, when the time available to recover from a setback shortens most quickly.

Age is the one input a risk profile cannot do without. Age is the one input a risk profile cannot do without. Until your date of birth is on file, the plan on your Invest page and your cashflow projection wait for it. The goal-based allocation in chat and suggestions for new money go ahead on a standard default score in the Moderately Aggressive band.

### Stage 2 — Capacity from your financial picture

We then adjust that starting point for the rest of your financial reality: how many years of living costs your cash and deposits could cover, and how much you owe outside of a home loan relative to that cushion. A strong cushion with little borrowing lifts capacity above what age alone would suggest; a thin or heavily borrowed one pulls it down. This reading uses the cash, deposits and bonds on your profile — your mutual-fund portfolio and directly held shares are not part of it — and neither a home you own nor a home loan enters it. It is also how what you owe reaches your investment allocation: borrowing lowers capacity, and through it the score, while loans are not deducted from the corpus the allocation invests. (Your cashflow projection is different: it nets debts other than a home loan off its starting corpus and runs a home loan as its monthly EMI.)

### Stage 3 — The savings-rate signal

How much of your income you save is read as independent evidence of resilience and recorded in the written summary stored with your risk profile — strong discipline is acknowledged, near-zero saving is noted as a caution. Today this signal shapes the narrative view of your profile; it does not move the capacity score or the downstream allocation.

### Stage 4 — Combine and categorise

- **Willingness** is read from what you tell us about your comfort with risk across four questions: your preferred risk-and-return trade-off at onboarding, your investing experience, whether your focus is safety or growth, and how you'd react to a sharp fall. We combine your preference, focus and drop-reaction, with a very cautious answer to any one of them holding the number close to that caution rather than being averaged away, and let your stated experience *cap* how high the number can climb — a self-described novice isn't scored as an aggressive risk-taker on strength of the other answers alone. If you've answered only some of these, we score from what you've given us; if you haven't answered any yet, we assume a middle-of-the-road willingness rather than blocking your plan.
- The final **risk score** combines willingness and capacity, with willingness carrying the greater weight and capacity acting as the anchor.
- When the two diverge meaningfully, we **record the gap** — a plain-language note in the written summary stored with your risk profile — so the tension is named rather than silently split down the middle.
- The score maps to a **category** — Conservative, Moderately Conservative, Moderate, Moderately Aggressive, or Aggressive — which is how we describe your investing style when we discuss your plan.

**Occupation stability** — steadier, more predictable incomes can support more growth exposure than variable or commission-based ones — is not something we ask about, so every profile carries the same standard assumption (salaried, private sector). It does not move the headline score or alter the portfolio.

## Where an explicit instruction outranks the score

The risk score decides what we *recommend*. It does not overrule what you *instruct*.

If you save an explicit investment preference on your preferences page — a specific split across equity, debt and commodity, and if you wish the categories within them — that split is what your practical plan is built to, even where it sits outside the range your risk score would otherwise have produced. We do not quietly clip your instruction back to the band and present the result as your plan. You asked for something specific; we build it — across the whole plan, so we do not also hold money back in steadier funds for goals due soon. Where something stops the split being met in full, we tell you why. A preference is set on that page, not in chat: ask chat for more equity or fewer mid caps and it points you there.

What we keep doing is telling you the truth about it. Our own recommendation is computed as if you had said nothing — from your risk score and the rest of your profile — and your preferences page shows it beside your own split, so you see both what we would advise and what you asked for. Where the two differ, the difference is in front of you rather than smoothed over, and when we discuss your plan in chat we say that your saved preference shaped it. That is the whole point of separating the score from the instruction — the score stays an honest opinion precisely because it is not bent to match your preference.

The one thing a preference does not do is change the score itself. Your risk profile continues to reflect your capacity and willingness as assessed, and it drives the recommendation shown beside your preference.

## Why a customer should trust this approach

| Question | Our answer |
| --- | --- |
| **Why is my score lower than I expected?** | Either your stated comfort with risk was lower than your gut suggests (worth re-checking), or your financial *capacity* is pulling the combined number down — typically because your cash and deposits cover only a short stretch of expenses, or because of debt. We can walk through which factor is doing the work. |
| **Why isn't my score equal to my stated appetite?** | Because appetite leads but does not rule. Your financial capacity always tethers the result, so a high appetite on a thin base lands somewhere in between — by design. The anchor stops a single confident answer from over-tilting an entire long-term portfolio. |
| **Why does willingness carry more weight than capacity?** | The portfolio has to be one you can hold through a sharp drawdown without panic-selling. Behavioural fit beats paper optimisation over a full cycle — so your preference leads. The capacity anchor is there to stop a preference the bank account can't support. |
| **What if my appetite and my capacity disagree?** | Then they differ by enough to matter. That gap is usually the most informative thing we know about you, and it cuts both ways — too much appetite for the base risks abandoning the plan in a downturn; too little for the base risks leaving returns on the table. We record the gap in the written summary stored with your risk profile, rather than letting it quietly shape your number with no explanation. |
| **My job is steady but the score didn't reward it — why?** | By design. The headline score is anchored to independently verifiable financial facts — age, expense coverage, debt — plus your stated appetite. We don't ask about occupation, so it doesn't move the score or reshape the plan. |
| **My risk category changed but I didn't change anything — why?** | Your profile recalculates whenever something that feeds it is updated — your profile details, your risk answers, a new statement upload — and each recalculation re-reads your age from your date of birth, so the capacity side can shift simply because a birthday has passed. Because the score maps to category bands, a small shift near a boundary can change the label while the underlying picture has barely moved. We can show you exactly which input moved. |
| **Why does my dashboard show a different risk profile from my plan?** | The Risk Profile label on your portfolio dashboard — and the risk profile chat reads back when you simply ask what yours is — reflects the risk-and-return option you chose at onboarding, on its own. Your plan is built on the combined score described here — willingness across all four questions, anchored by capacity — and that score's category is the one we use when we walk through your allocation. The two can differ; when they do, the plan follows the combined score. |
| **Can I just pick a different risk score?** | Your saved score always comes from your answers and your finances; it is not set by hand. Update your risk answers or financial details on your profile and it recalculates. In a conversation about your plan you can ask to see it at a different score — that comes back as a what-if beside your saved plan, and nothing is saved. |
| **Why didn't you ask me more questions about my appetite?** | We keep the appetite input deliberately simple. More questions don't reliably produce better scores — they often add noise unless each question is independently validated. Capacity, by contrast, draws on several independent and verifiable facts about your finances. We'll add to the appetite instrument only when we have hard evidence it adds signal. |

## What this thesis is — and is not

This document is a directional reference. It is not a prediction, not a guarantee that the resulting allocation suits every life event, and not a substitute for talking to us directly if a gap between your appetite and your capacity concerns you. The way we weigh these factors is reviewed periodically and may evolve as we gather behavioural evidence. When it does, this thesis is updated and dated.
