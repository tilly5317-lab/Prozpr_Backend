## The one-line thesis

Risk is two questions that look like one. *Can you afford to take this risk?* — a function of your age, your assets, what you owe, and what you own. *Do you want to take this risk?* — a function of your temperament, your experience and your stated comfort with drawdowns. Neither answer by itself builds a real portfolio: a young saver with no appetite will under-invest for decades; an older investor with a large appetite will over-invest into a market they cannot wait out. We assess both, combine them in a way that respects your stated preference while keeping it anchored to your financial reality, and where the two disagree by enough to matter, we say so plainly in your profile summary — not to gate what happens next, but so you go in with your eyes open.

## Five principles that drive every risk score

| Principle | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Assess ability and willingness separately** | We form two independent views on the same scale: a **risk-capacity** view (purely financial — age, how long your assets could cover expenses, what you owe, what property you hold) and a **risk-willingness** view (your stated comfort with risk). Keeping them separate lets us tell you *which* of the two is driving your final number — and where the disagreement lies if there is one. |
| **2. Age is the spine of capacity, not the whole skeleton** | A younger investor has decades to ride out a drawdown; someone near retirement does not. Age sets the base of your capacity, tapering as the retirement window approaches. But age alone is too crude — a young investor with dependants, no savings and a credit-card balance has less room to ride a drawdown than age suggests, and vice versa. So your broader financial picture lifts or lowers that starting point. |
| **3. Savings discipline is a quiet but powerful signal** | We read the share of income you actually save as evidence of your ability to absorb volatility, and we call it out in how we describe your profile: strong, consistent saving is acknowledged; saving almost nothing is a caution flag, not a feature; a slim-but-positive margin is simply neutral. Today this signal informs the narrative view of your profile — it does not move the score itself. |
| **4. Willingness leads, capacity tethers** | Your stated preference carries the greater weight in the final score, because the portfolio has to be one *you can actually live with through a sharp drawdown* — a paper-optimal portfolio you panic-sell at the bottom is worse than a paper-suboptimal one you hold. But capacity always tethers the result: a high appetite sitting on a thin financial base will not produce a maximally aggressive score, because your stomach should not override your bank account. |
| **5. Flag the gap, don't paper over it** | When your willingness and your capacity diverge meaningfully, we say so explicitly in your profile summary — kindly, as a nudge rather than a warning. It's one of the most useful things we can tell you: a large appetite on a thin base is the profile most likely to abandon the plan at the worst possible time, and the inverse is the profile most likely to leave decades of compounding on the table. |

We are a risk-profiling approach, not a personality test and not a brokerage suitability form. The output is one number, traceable to the factors that produced it, built to inform how your portfolio is shaped — nothing more.

## How a risk score is built — at a high level

Every Ask PI risk profile moves through four deliberate stages. We can walk through the reasoning behind any of them on demand.

### Stage 1 — Capacity from age

We start with age, because time-to-need is the single biggest driver of how much volatility you can afford. Capacity starts higher for younger investors and tapers as the retirement window draws closer — and it tapers fastest through the years right around retirement, when the time available to recover from a setback shortens most quickly.

### Stage 2 — Capacity from your financial picture

We then adjust that starting point for the rest of your financial reality: how many years of living costs your liquid assets could cover, how much you owe outside of a home loan (though the monthly servicing of a home loan still counts against your capacity), and what property you own. A strong balance sheet lifts capacity above what age alone would suggest; a stretched one pulls it down.

### Stage 3 — The savings-rate signal

How much of your income you save is read as independent evidence of resilience and called out in your profile summary — strong discipline is acknowledged, near-zero saving is a caution flag. Today this signal shapes the narrative view of your profile; it does not move the capacity score or the downstream allocation.

### Stage 4 — Combine, flag and categorise

- **Willingness** comes from what you tell us at onboarding about your comfort with risk. If you haven't answered the appetite questions yet, we assume a middle-of-the-road willingness rather than blocking your plan.
- The final **risk score** combines willingness and capacity, with willingness carrying the greater weight and capacity acting as the anchor.
- When the two diverge meaningfully, we **raise a flag** — a plain-language note in your profile summary — so you see the tension named rather than silently split down the middle.
- The score maps to a **category** — Conservative, Moderately Conservative, Moderate, Moderately Aggressive, or Aggressive — which is the headline we and you reason about.

We also form a view on **occupation stability** — steadier, more predictable incomes can support more growth exposure than variable or commission-based ones. This view is recorded as context on your profile; it deliberately does not move the headline score, and today it does not alter the portfolio either. It is a signal we intend to put to work as the approach evolves.

## Why a customer should trust this approach

| Question | Our answer |
| --- | --- |
| **Why is my score lower than I expected?** | Either your stated comfort with risk was lower than your gut suggests (worth re-checking), or your financial *capacity* is pulling the combined number down — typically because of short expense coverage, debt, or no property. We can walk through which factor is doing the work. |
| **Why isn't my score equal to my stated appetite?** | Because appetite leads but does not rule. Your financial capacity always tethers the result, so a high appetite on a thin base lands somewhere in between — by design. The anchor stops a single confident answer from over-tilting an entire long-term portfolio. |
| **Why does willingness carry more weight than capacity?** | The portfolio has to be one you can hold through a sharp drawdown without panic-selling. Behavioural fit beats paper optimisation over a full cycle — so your preference leads. The capacity anchor is there to stop a preference the bank account can't support. |
| **Why did you flag a "gap" on my profile?** | Your stated appetite and your financial capacity differ by enough to matter. That gap is usually the most informative thing we know about you, and it cuts both ways — too much appetite for the base risks abandoning the plan in a downturn; too little for the base risks leaving returns on the table. The flag exists so you see it named plainly, rather than it quietly shaping your number with no explanation. |
| **My job is steady but the score didn't reward it — why?** | By design. The headline score is anchored to independently verifiable financial facts — age, expense coverage, debt, property — plus your stated appetite. Occupation stability is recorded as context on your profile, but it doesn't move the score, and it doesn't currently reshape the plan either. It's a signal we intend to put to work as the approach evolves. |
| **My risk category changed but I didn't change anything — why?** | Your profile refreshes when its inputs refresh — and age is always current, so the capacity side can shift simply because a birthday has passed. Because the score maps to category bands, a small shift near a boundary can change the label while the underlying picture has barely moved. We can show you exactly which input moved. |
| **Why didn't you ask me more questions about my appetite?** | We keep the appetite input deliberately simple. More questions don't reliably produce better scores — they often add noise unless each question is independently validated. Capacity, by contrast, draws on several independent and verifiable facts about your finances. We'll add to the appetite instrument only when we have hard evidence it adds signal. |

## What this thesis is — and is not

This document is a directional reference. It is not a prediction, not a guarantee that the resulting allocation suits every life event, and not a substitute for talking to us directly if a flagged gap concerns you. The way we weigh these factors is reviewed periodically and may evolve as we gather behavioural evidence. When it does, this thesis is updated and dated.
